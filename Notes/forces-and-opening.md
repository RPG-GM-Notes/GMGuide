<!-- (dl (section-meta Campaigns as a Physics Engine Rough)) -->

I treat campaign creation as building a physics engine: I define a small number of forces and set their actions in motion. A force adds energy to a system that cannot be ignored; it can move the world around the characters. Power alone is not enough to make someone a force. A force must meet all four criteria:

- It is more powerful than the characters. What “more powerful” means depends on the current story and what I want to emphasize; that balance can shift incrementally as play changes the force.
- It has a goal it is trying to achieve.
- It has lines it will and will not cross.
- It has a fingerprint: findable evidence that lets the players recognize separate actions as the work of the same person or force. It might be a method, a trace left behind, how the force treats people, or something else. It can be deliberate or incidental, and can be loosely defined.

A force might be a tribe of were-gators or a single Nephondi High Mage. A group can count as one character at the force level while its members remain distinct individuals; for example, I treated all the biker gangs as one character for the force while letting individuals be individuals. When members act toward the force’s goal, they act as the force; when they act toward something else, I treat them individually.

If members stop pursuing the shared goal or disagree about it, I decide whether the force changes, splits, or its members act individually based on what the story calls for.

<!-- (dl (# Preparing Forces and Theme)) -->

Defining good forces is difficult and takes time. I usually spend about 70% of my initial preparation building the Stage and about 20% building the forces. I brainstorm and spitball until I find ideas I like, often starting with a character idea but not always. There is no fixed sequence for developing a force’s goal, fingerprint, limits, or character.

A good force needs to play into the campaign’s theme and give me many options for play. I also consider what kind of character it is. There is no fixed order for developing forces and theme: I may start with either and loop back and forth until I have something I like. What matters is having both. I also build a few supporting characters.

Supporting characters help tell the story I want: a thematic or emotional through-line, not a predetermined sequence of events. They can connect the characters to forces, be affected by forces, or act independently. For example, the drug-addicted friend had no direct tie to any force, though forces hovered around him incidentally; he helped tell a story about institutionalized pain.

I map force relationships up front, including alliances, rivalries, obligations, history, and dependencies. I also consider how local groups view each force: who hates it, who would hate it if they knew its identity, and who loves it. I distinguish what people know from what they believe, including what forces know or believe about one another, but keep those notes light—usually a sentence or three. The more I know about a force, the easier it is to make something up on the fly.

<!-- (dl (# Play Through Force Reactions)) -->

This is an improvisational system that relies on substantial initial preparation. The preparation builds a model for making decisions on the fly; much of what is prepared may never be used. Nothing has to be predetermined. I may occasionally decide on something in advance if I have an idea, but I hold it loosely and do so rarely.

The goal is to provide entertainment. I let the players dictate what is entertaining and follow their lead. I do not focus solely on what was originally planned; everything can be disrupted and changed. The purpose of a force is to aid improvisation, not to chain the campaign to a predetermined direction.

I do not care what the outcome is before it becomes real. I keep the thematic tone by adding conditions, responses, or side effects to outcomes, as long as they do not invalidate a character’s choice. The outcome stays open while the tone remains intentional.

After the first inciting incident, what happens is driven by how the forces react to the players. The forces are trying to accomplish their goals, and the players interfere with those efforts. The forces then react. Knowing which lines a force will cross and which it will not determines the limits of its response.

I keep the number of active forces small; three is a useful rule of thumb. A force is dealt with when it is no longer more powerful than the characters. A force can also stop being a force because the players stop engaging with it. What happens to a force then depends on what seems most interesting. I try to seek out what entertains the players.

Disengagement is usually obvious: the players stop reacting, responding, or engaging with what the force is doing. Sometimes they will directly say they do not like a thread; that is usually a bad sign.

I mostly track active forces through session notes. I annotate the force notes when there is something specific I need to preserve, such as a change in goals, resources, or lines, or a change to any of the force’s four defining criteria. Forces can gain or lose anything, depending on the story.

Whether a changed force still feels like the same force depends on context. If the characters know the person or organization, continuity is easier to recognize. If they do not know it, the changed force may not feel like the same one.

<!-- (dl (# The Opening Collision)) -->

I find an intersection where the actions of two or more forces come into contact. That intersection becomes the opening scene, and I always start the game with combat. The combat is a result of colliding forces, but the characters do not have to be fighting the forces directly: they may be caught in the fallout of those forces’ actions.

I start with combat because most games have their most developed and detailed rules for it. That gives players the best opportunity to try out their new characters using the part of the game the rules support most heavily.

The collision must tie meaningfully into something the characters would be involved in. Most often, I plan the opening scene and give the players the things that make it work, though I have also built the scene the other way around.

In one campaign, every character had a police officer as a contact. That connection existed before the fight, but I explained after combat that it happened because the contact had asked them to look into something. Revealing the reason afterward makes the exposition easier to stomach. For that opening, I gave everyone a free advantage because I knew what I wanted the scene to be.

<!-- (dl (# Examples)) -->

<!-- (dl (## Gang Fight and the Cartel Daughter)) -->

In one mage game, the opening combat was a gang fight, fallout from a Cartel Daughter trying to take over the city and its street gangs. Her fingerprint came from desperate paranoia: everything she did was over-planned, with contingencies layered on contingencies.

The opening involved earlier, local forces. The were-gator tribe and Nephondi High Mage did not appear until those earlier forces were dealt with. The conflict with the gangs unfolded over eight sessions. The players did not realize they were taking out the gang leaders until it was over; this was an effect of what happened, not their main goal. The first leader was killed for revenge, the second by accident, and the third in self-defense. I do not remember what happened with the fourth or fifth. By the end of the eighth session, I had decided the Cartel Daughter would gain control of the city. The players had not known about her or her attempt to take over. When they discovered the new situation, they had to decide how to handle her.

<!-- (dl (## The Nephondi High Mage)) -->

The Nephondi High Mage had the opposite kind of fingerprint: a lack of one. Things happened without any way to trace them back to him. The players found several untraceable things, such as documents they were looking for that were delivered by people paid $200 to take them to “that house.” Some of the information in these deliveries was based on things only the High Mage knew, but the players never figured out that he was behind them. They still talk about the unsolved puzzle fondly.

He was a master manipulator and liar who deeply hated all people, including himself, but took pride in the destruction caused by his long game. His goal was to corrupt a powerful spirit dormant beneath the city and sacrifice it to create a wyrm pit. The spirit’s power had created three different nodes within the city.

He would not use magic near another mage, because a mage sensitive to corruption might expose him. As a result, everything he did that interacted with the players was mundane. He befriended the players and fed them information they needed, so they ended up calling him their most trusted contact despite having evidence to the contrary. He could also sow discord within the party without making it look intentional.

The campaign’s theme was “Institutional Harm lives for generations.” The Nephondi killed a young mage he was corrupting. That death affected another character, who fell into drug abuse and, twenty years later, was contemplating suicide. The murdered mage had become a wraith and was trying to save his friend through the player characters. The wraith was weak, so he was a supporting character rather than a force. The Nephondi lost his plan and his life.

<!-- (dl (## The Mayor)) -->

In a street-level superhero campaign, the mayor was a force whose fingerprint was that everything was documented and traceable through paper. The mayor gained national recognition.
