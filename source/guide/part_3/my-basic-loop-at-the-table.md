<!-- (dl (section-meta 7. My Basic Loop at the Table)) -->

<!-- (dl (# The Loop)) -->

At the table, I keep returning to a simple loop: I present what is happening, find out what the players want to do, decide what needs resolution, establish what changes, and give the situation back to them. This is not a script or a set of steps I have to perform mechanically. It is a way to keep play responsive: the players can act on what they see, and the world can change in response.

<!-- (dl (# Present What Is Happening)) -->

I describe the part of the situation the characters can perceive and act on. That might be what a person is doing, what has changed because of an earlier action, or the immediate effects of a force pursuing its goal. I give enough information for the players to understand what their characters could reasonably notice, but I do not need to explain the whole Stage or every cause behind the moment.

If the characters have questions, I answer from what they know, what they can discover, and what is actually true in the world. Sometimes the players' questions are the best cue for what background to explain. I want them to learn about the situation because they are interested in it, not because I have stopped play to deliver everything I prepared.

<!-- (dl (# Find Out What the Players Want to Do)) -->

I let the players decide what their characters attempt. If I am unsure what a player means, I ask what the character is trying to accomplish or how they intend to approach it. That gives me a better basis for deciding what happens.

<!-- (dl (# Decide What Needs Resolution)) -->

Once I understand the intention, I decide whether anything needs to be resolved. Some actions are clear from the situation and need no roll or extended discussion. When the outcome is uncertain, I ask whether failure would make the current story more interesting; I roll only if it would. Otherwise, I resolve the action without a roll. I can use a ruling or talk through the situation with the players when that helps. How I choose between these approaches, and how I handle a difficult ruling, belongs to the next chapter.

<!-- (dl (# Establish What Changes)) -->

I first ask, “What is the most interesting outcome that can happen within the campaign's theme?” Then I work out how it can grow from the action and the situation already established: the Stage, the Forces and their goals and limits, and the relationships in play. If I cannot find a grounded way for that outcome to happen, I look for another thematic possibility rather than forcing it. Theme leads what I consider; the established situation helps me make the outcome belong. Chapter 10 covers how to develop and reveal that fallout.

<!-- (dl (# Give the Situation Back to the Players)) -->

I make the change visible, then return the focus to the players. They need to see enough of what happened to choose what their characters do next. That may mean showing the immediate result, describing a reaction, or revealing a clue to a larger problem. I do not need to explain every implication at once; the players can investigate, ask questions, or move in another direction.

Their next choice begins the loop again. The forces react to what the characters have changed, and the world develops from the actions at the table. I use the loop to keep play moving while leaving the outcome open.
