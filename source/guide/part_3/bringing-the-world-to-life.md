<!-- (dl (# Deciding What to Describe)) -->

I try to give at least two concrete details for each scene and for every NPC or group of NPCs. I describe enough of the background for people to understand the situation, then let their minds fill in the rest. The details do not need to be elaborate. They need to give the players something specific to picture and act on.

The theme helps me decide which details to foreground and how to frame them. Two scenes can contain the same facts but feel different depending on what the description emphasizes.

For example:

> As you wait in the dimly lit parking lot of a medium-sized church, three rough-looking men ride up on Harley-Davidson motorcycles. They are the remaining heads of the local gangs, and they look pissed.

That is the whole lead-up description. The parking lot and church establish the scene; the men, their motorcycles, and their anger make the arrival concrete. The players know who has arrived and what kind of moment this is without needing a long description of the lot, the building, or each man's appearance. I give them enough to imagine the scene, then leave space for their own picture of it.

<!-- (dl (# Portraying People and Their Motivations)) -->

I want NPCs to feel like people with reasons for what they do, not pieces I move around to deliver information or serve a plot. Every NPC has goals. Those goals are often immediate and obvious, and they govern the NPC's actions and interactions with the characters.

An NPC can still serve a purpose in the campaign. In one game, I had a woman who was meant to give the players information and demonstrate one of the forces' power. She was also trying to save her son, who was a prisoner of war. If she succeeded, he would live free; if she failed, he would remain a POW. That goal shaped every interaction she had with the characters. Her campaign role did not replace her own reason to act.

NPCs also have emotions. Most of the time, these are not explosive or theatrical, but they still affect how people respond. If the characters insult an NPC, that person might act injured or self-righteous, depending on who they are and what they want. The goal gives the interaction direction; the emotion gives it a human texture.

In combat, I make sure at least one NPC has a name and is called by it. A name gives the fight weight and draws attention to the fact that these are people. If I have the time and inspiration while preparing, I name all the combatants.

<!-- (dl (# Responding to What the Characters Do)) -->

The characters' significant actions change the situation. My first question is, “What is the most interesting outcome that can happen within the campaign's theme?” I start there, rather than first listing likely consequences and only afterward looking for a thematic one. Then I work out how that outcome can grow from what the characters did and from the Stage, Forces, goals, limits, and relationships already in play. If I cannot find a grounded way for it to happen, I reconsider the outcome rather than forcing it into the situation.

After that, I ask what else follows. I expect a significant act to have at least two consequences: one often immediate, another perhaps several sessions later. These further effects should grow from the same changed situation, not become a quota of extra threads to maintain. Consequences can be beneficial, harmful, or mixed; they should deepen the situation, not punish the players or erase their success. The thematic outcome gives the fallout its focus, while the established world gives it a way to happen.

Suppose the campaign asks, “Can a community become safe without reproducing violence?” If the characters kill the head of a local gang, one interesting outcome is that people the leader hurt thank them and offer to help make the neighborhood safer, while removing the leader destabilizes the underground power structure and increases gang violence. That puts the question back into play through a real change: the characters have helped people, and the power vacuum creates a new pressure around what safety will mean. Both results grow from the same act; neither erases what the characters accomplished or dictates what they should do next.

<!-- (dl (# Making Consequences Visible)) -->

I want the players to encounter the effects of their choices, even when those effects are not immediately obvious. Consequences can show up in changed circumstances, other people's behavior, or details of ordinary life—not only through an NPC explaining what the characters caused.

In the earlier campaign that inspired the example in Chapter 14, the characters were trying to find the cause of a massacre that had happened twenty years earlier. The Mokolé were openly buying land, and their goals collided with the Vampires' interests, setting off a real-estate war. Land prices soared and housing costs rose so sharply that the news began reporting on apartments where large numbers of people were sharing rooms.

That kind of ripple makes the characters important even if the world does not know why things are happening. A news report can reveal the larger effect without explaining every link in the chain. In another game, stopping an evil corporation meant that its factory site eventually became a park. Fallout can be a lasting improvement, not just a new problem.

<!-- (dl (# Keeping the World Coherent as It Changes)) -->

Let consequences grow from the established situation. Effects may be indirect or take time to appear; show what changed and let the players discover its connection if they investigate.
