<!-- (dl (# Deciding What to Describe)) -->

I try to give at least two concrete details for each scene and for every NPC or group of NPCs. I describe enough of the background for people to understand the situation, then let their minds fill in the rest. The details do not need to be elaborate. They need to give the players something specific to picture and act on.

For example:

> As you wait in the dimly lit parking lot of a medium-sized church, three rough-looking men ride up on Harley-Davidson motorcycles. They are the remaining heads of the local gangs, and they look pissed.

That is the whole lead-up description. The parking lot and church establish the scene; the men, their motorcycles, and their anger make the arrival concrete. The players know who has arrived and what kind of moment this is without needing a long description of the lot, the building, or each man's appearance. I give them enough to imagine the scene, then leave space for their own picture of it.

<!-- (dl (# Portraying People and Their Motivations)) -->

I want NPCs to feel like people with reasons for what they do, not pieces I move around to deliver information or serve a plot. Every NPC has goals. Those goals are often immediate and obvious, and they govern the NPC's actions and interactions with the characters.

An NPC can still serve a purpose in the campaign. In one game, I had a woman who was meant to give the players information and demonstrate one of the forces' power. She was also trying to save her son, who was a prisoner of war. If she succeeded, he would live free; if she failed, he would remain a POW. That goal shaped every interaction she had with the characters. Her campaign role did not replace her own reason to act.

NPCs also have emotions. Most of the time, these are not explosive or theatrical, but they still affect how people respond. If the characters insult an NPC, that person might act injured or self-righteous, depending on who they are and what they want. The goal gives the interaction direction; the emotion gives it a human texture.

In combat, I make sure at least one NPC has a name and is called by it. A name gives the fight weight and draws attention to the fact that these are people. If I have the time and inspiration while preparing, I name all the combatants.

<!-- (dl (# Responding to What the Characters Do)) -->

The characters' significant actions change the situation. I do not decide in advance what those actions must lead to; I look at what follows from what the characters actually did and from the people, forces, and relationships already in the game.

I expect every significant act to have at least two consequences, and sometimes more. One is usually fairly immediate. Another may follow from the way the wider situation adjusts. The consequences can point in different directions: an action can help some people while creating trouble elsewhere. They are not a way to take back a success or punish the characters for acting. They are how the world responds.

If the characters kill the head of a local gang, the people that person hurt may thank them. At the same time, removing that leader can destabilize the underground power structure and increase gang violence. Both results follow from the same act. The characters accomplished something real, and the situation has changed in ways they may now have to deal with.

<!-- (dl (# Making Consequences Visible)) -->

I want the players to encounter the effects of their choices, even when those effects are not immediately obvious. Consequences do not have to arrive as an NPC explaining what the characters caused. They can show up in changed circumstances, other people's behavior, or details of ordinary life.

In one game, the characters were trying to find the cause of a massacre that had happened twenty years earlier. In doing so, they accidentally started a real-estate war. Land prices soared and housing costs rose so sharply that the news began reporting on apartments where large numbers of people were sharing rooms.

That kind of ripple makes the characters important even if the world does not know why things are happening. The people in those apartments do not need to know that the characters' investigation helped cause the changes. The characters' actions still reach beyond the people they meet directly, and a news report can make that larger effect visible without stopping to explain every link in the chain.

<!-- (dl (# Keeping the World Coherent as It Changes)) -->

The world can change in ways I did not plan, but I want those changes to grow out of what is already happening. I use the Stage, the forces' goals and limits, their relationships, and the actions people have taken to work out what might follow. That gives me a basis for improvising without having to predict the characters' choices.

Sometimes a consequence will be indirect or take time to appear. I do not need to reveal the whole chain at once. I can let the players see a new effect first, then discover its connection as they ask questions or investigate. The characters may never learn every connection, and the wider world may never recognize their part in what changed.

The point is not to make the world react to every choice in a way that announces, “This was because of you.” It is to let the characters' choices matter. The world can respond, adapt, and keep moving around them, whether or not anyone understands why.
