<!-- (dl (# What Persists Between Sessions)) -->

Between sessions, the campaign's current state is the record of what has changed (see Chapter 11). Forces continue pursuing their goals within their limits, and their goals, resources, and relationships can change. The Stage changes too; character actions may affect people or places far beyond their immediate targets.

I also update a Force's notes when play reveals a fingerprint different from the one I prepared. Its behavior may have settled into an unplanned signature, while the intended one never appeared. I record the pattern that actually emerged and use it going forward, rather than forcing the original signature into later scenes.

<!-- (dl (# Following Player Interests)) -->

Over the campaign, I follow the interests that persist and let others recede. The world does not stop when the characters look elsewhere: forces keep pursuing their goals, but I do not keep presenting a thread just because I prepared it. The theme's question remains present through the situations the campaign creates, but it does not require the players to pursue a particular thread. I use the theme to build on their interests and offer new angles for exploration without steering them toward a predetermined answer.

<!-- (dl (## Distinguishing Curiosity from Investment)) -->

Curiosity says, "I want to know more." Players may ask about someone, investigate an odd detail, or follow a lead to see what is there. That earns an answer and room to explore, not an assumption that this interest is now central to the campaign.

Investment says, "What happens here matters to me." The players begin wanting something for or from a person, place, or situation, and their involvement influences their decisions. I infer that commitment from play rather than deciding in advance what they should care about.

Signs that curiosity is becoming investment include:

- **Returning without prompting.** The players bring it up again or choose to go back when other options are available.
- **Taking initiative.** They form their own goal rather than only responding to information I offer.
- **Giving it priority.** They devote time, resources, or effort to it, or let it affect how they approach another problem.
- **Caring about the outcome.** They want someone protected, something changed, a relationship maintained, or a wrong answered—not merely a mystery explained.

Time spent is useful evidence, but not sufficient by itself. Players may spend a whole session investigating something because it is difficult to understand, while a brief encounter may produce immediate attachment. Investment does not require a sacrifice; caring can be evident before it costs anything. It does not require affection either: someone the players distrust, a place they want to escape, or a problem they are determined to end can become an emotional center.

Curiosity is a reason to offer more; investment is a reason to build more deeply. I give the players enough to discover whether they care. If they do, I develop connections and consequences around what they have made important. If they do not, I let it remain a passing interest.

<!-- (dl (## Accumulating Meaning, Direction, and an Emotional Center)) -->

Following what catches the players' eye does not mean resetting the campaign around every new curiosity. I develop their interests in relation to the Stage, the Forces, the theme, and what has already happened. Exploration becomes cumulative because the world they encounter has already been changed by their earlier choices.

Meaning accumulates through choices and their consequences. The theme supplies a question or idea; the campaign's situations keep presenting it from different angles, and play gives it lived examples. What the characters protect, sacrifice, accept, or refuse gradually makes that question personal without requiring a predetermined answer.

Direction accumulates through commitments. A curiosity can become an investigation, an investigation can become a relationship or goal, and pursuing that goal changes the situation. Earlier decisions create reasons for later decisions. The direction can change, but it has a history.

An emotional center accumulates through repeated involvement. A person, place, relationship, or struggle comes to matter because the players have spent time with it and changed things through it. It carries memories of what they accomplished, lost, or still hope to do.

My job is to build on what has become important, not merely supply the next interesting thing. A new development can deepen an existing relationship, reveal another side of an earlier choice, or connect a current concern to an older one; it does not always need to open another thread. Some interests fade, while others become commitments. I follow the players' attention without discarding what their involvement has already made meaningful.

<!-- (dl (# Revisiting Unresolved Situations)) -->

An unresolved situation may return if it still matters to the characters or the world; it should not return merely because it was introduced. An Arc may end when its fallout reaches a natural conclusion, or when the players disengage. The latter lets play move on, but it is not closure and does not count as a completed thread when I consider the campaign's ending.

<!-- (dl (# Changing Direction)) -->

The campaign's direction can emerge from connections I did not plan: a force may matter across several Arcs, or a character's action may set off changes far beyond the immediate situation. I let those developments reshape the angles from which the theme is explored, and may refine my understanding of the question as play reveals what matters to the group. I preserve enough continuity for those developments to feel earned, while leaving the moral meaning and conclusions open to the players.

<!-- (dl (# Recognizing When a Campaign Should End)) -->

A campaign can outlive itself. It is important to end it deliberately instead of letting it simply die from neglect or lost momentum. The campaigns that end are the ones people continue to talk about; the ones that just die tend to disappear from memory.

I look for a point where enough completed threads can connect to the current ones. When one or more active threads reach their end, those connections can make the ending feel satisfying. The skill is finding the right moment to draw things together neatly, but not completely. There should still be loose ends, while the characters have clearly accomplished something major. For me, that ending is usually bittersweet.

In one game, the players discovered that a wizard they had thought was their friend was actually an evil force. He had been active across a good number of their stories. The players fought and defeated him. He was one of three forces in the campaign, but his defeat was the point where I called the campaign over. It surprised the whole table, and they still talk about that ending when they get together.

I had not waited for every thread or force to be resolved. The wizard's defeat connected to what the players had experienced, gave them a major accomplishment, and felt like a place to stop. The campaign ended with loose ends because the moment already felt complete.
