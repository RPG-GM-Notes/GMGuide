<!-- (dl (# Finding a Stopping Point)) -->

Most of my games are timebound. We agree when we start when the session will end, and I plan to respect that time. My hard rule is not to end while the characters are in the middle of doing something. I look for a point between activities: before or after combat, a political scene, an intrigue, or an investigation.

I love cliffhangers, but I do not plan for them or try to encourage them. If a good one happens naturally, I am often willing to end a few minutes early or late to use it. I will not stretch the session by more than about fifteen minutes for that. A cliffhanger is a welcome stopping point, not a reason to push the players toward one or disregard the time we agreed on.

<!-- (dl (# What I Record Afterward)) -->

After a session, I note what the players did, the characters' significant actions, what changed for each force and the Stage, and any rewards. I do not need a transcript—just a record of changes likely to matter again.

<!-- (dl (## Keeping Fallout Manageable)) -->

My record keeping is proportional to the time the players spend involved with something, not to the number of consequences I can imagine. If fallout becomes a minor note, I let it remain a minor note. It may eventually vanish from memory without needing a formal resolution. If the players spend more time engaging with it, I develop and record more of it.

Fallout becomes bloated when I treat every plausible consequence as something I must create, track, and eventually show. Expecting multiple consequences from a significant action is not a requirement to maintain a separate thread for each one. I do not need to follow every ripple recursively, give every affected actor an ongoing response, or keep updating an effect after it has served its purpose.

A consequence can become a fact without becoming a thread. If an abandoned factory becomes a park, that is lasting fallout; it does not require a park-management subplot. A change to a Force's influence or relationships can go into that Force's notes rather than becoming a separate chain of obligations. I ask what I need to retain to respond well in future play, not how much more I could calculate.

Remaining true in the world is different from remaining active in my records. If the players return to a minor consequence, I can develop it then, drawing on what they remember and what is established. Fallout creates possibilities for play; player involvement determines how much of it becomes maintained material.

<!-- (dl (# What I Ask the Players)) -->

After every session, I ask the players, “How could this be better?” I listen and record their advice, but I do not take each suggestion at face value or immediately treat it as an instruction to change something.

I compare feedback across sessions and look for patterns. If I start to see one, I open a session with a discussion about it. I want to understand why the players are making those suggestions. The reason behind the feedback matters more to me than the particular fix someone proposes. Once I understand what is not working for them, we can talk about what would address it.

<!-- (dl (# Turning the Session into Useful Preparation)) -->

Those notes feed into the next session's preparation (see Chapter 6). I use them to understand what changed and what the characters may need to engage with, not to plan what they will do.

The first four sessions are an exception. At that point, the characters may have done too little to propel the campaign and create much fallout of their own. I pay close attention to what has engaged the players and use those apparent interests to create inciting incidents that may appeal to them. As the characters' actions begin to change the situation, those actions and their effects become the main material feeding into the next session.

In my experience, the second session is almost always the least exciting session of a campaign. The first session has the momentum of its opening combat and the players' discovery of what the campaign is. By the second, that initial energy has faded, but the characters' actions may not yet have created enough fallout to move the campaign on its own. The GM is supplying more of the fuel, while the players are still getting familiar with the characters they created and learning what those characters might do in different situations. By the third session, player choices often begin to build momentum, and the players have a better feel for their characters.

I have not found a reliable way to make the second session exciting. Knowing this pattern helps me avoid treating a flat session as proof that I have failed or that the campaign is already failing. I keep responding to what has interested the players, and give their choices time to start shaping what comes next. This is not a reason to ignore genuine disinterest or a mismatch; it is a reminder that the campaign may still be finding its momentum.
