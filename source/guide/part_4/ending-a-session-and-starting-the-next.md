<!-- (dl (# Finding a Stopping Point)) -->

Most of my games are timebound. We agree when we start when the session will end, and I plan to respect that time. My hard rule is not to end while the characters are in the middle of doing something. I look for a point between activities: before or after combat, a political scene, an intrigue, or an investigation.

I love cliffhangers, but I do not plan for them or try to encourage them. If a good one happens naturally, I am often willing to end a few minutes early or late to use it. I will not stretch the session by more than about fifteen minutes for that. A cliffhanger is a welcome stopping point, not a reason to push the players toward one or disregard the time we agreed on.

<!-- (dl (# What I Record Afterward)) -->

After a session, I note what the players did, the characters' significant actions, what changed for each force and the Stage, and any rewards. I do not need a transcript—just a record of changes likely to matter again.

<!-- (dl (# What I Ask the Players)) -->

After every session, I ask the players, “How could this be better?” I listen and record their advice, but I do not take each suggestion at face value or immediately treat it as an instruction to change something.

I compare feedback across sessions and look for patterns. If I start to see one, I open a session with a discussion about it. I want to understand why the players are making those suggestions. The reason behind the feedback matters more to me than the particular fix someone proposes. Once I understand what is not working for them, we can talk about what would address it.

<!-- (dl (# Turning the Session into Useful Preparation)) -->

Those notes feed into preparation for the next session: I ask what interesting fallout might follow and prepare to respond to the changed situation.

The first four sessions are an exception. At that point, the characters may have done too little to propel the campaign and create much fallout of their own. I pay close attention to what has engaged the players and use those apparent interests to create inciting incidents that may appeal to them. As the characters' actions begin to change the situation, those actions and their effects become the main material feeding into the next session.
