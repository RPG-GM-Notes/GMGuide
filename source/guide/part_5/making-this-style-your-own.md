<!-- (dl (section-meta 15. Making This Style Your Own)) -->

You do not need to run games exactly as I do. Some parts of this approach are important to how it works; others are my preferences and rules of thumb. The goal is to understand what each part is doing so you can adapt it to your group and still get the kind of play you want.

<!-- (dl (# What Is Essential to the Approach)) -->

The essential part is a connected situation: the Stage gives actions context, forces create movement, and the characters can change both. You can scale the scope to fit your campaign, but safety and meaningful player choice are not optional techniques to set aside.

<!-- (dl (# Build a Small but Complete System)) -->

Starting small means building a bounded system with enough connected parts to work—not just one force without a Stage. Focus on one city, one neighborhood, or one part of a larger campaign; give the characters room to explore and the forces' actions context.

Put details from the Stage into conversations, places, and rumors. You do not need to explain everything up front. Let the players encounter the material, notice what interests them, and show you where they want to look more closely. Then develop forces in relation to the Stage and to that interest.

I often start with forces because their goals help me discover what the Stage needs, but the two develop together. Choose whichever gives you a useful way into the work.

<!-- (dl (# Try a Short Shot)) -->

You can also experiment by running a short campaign rather than planning a long-running one. I call a game intended to last five or fewer sessions a “Short Shot.” A short campaign benefits from a driving goal that gives the limited run direction. It will not be as free-form as a long campaign, and that can be fine given the short time available.

A driving goal gives the group something to engage with; it does not decide how the characters pursue it or require a particular outcome. Keep player agency and safety intact. The campaign can be focused without turning into a script.

For a Short Shot, I have found it useful to provide the players with starting characters and let them put whatever personality they want into them. Character creation can be a lot of effort for a game that will only last a few sessions. Pre-made characters can also make it easier to begin with a group that can work together for the short campaign.

That is a rule of thumb, not a rule. Your players may enjoy making characters, or may already have a cohesive group concept that fits the game. Choose the approach that serves the people at your table.

<!-- (dl (# How to Tell Whether It Is Working)) -->

After a few sessions, ask whether the players have found something to explore, whether their actions change the situation, and whether the group enjoys the play. If the Stage feels empty, add relationships, history, or pressures; if an unexpected interest emerges, develop it. You need not copy my preparation ratios, opening combat, or number of forces. Keep what helps your table and adjust the rest.
