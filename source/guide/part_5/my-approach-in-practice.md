<!-- (dl (section-meta 14. My Approach in Practice)) -->

This example follows a Mage: The Ascension 20th Anniversary Edition campaign with multiple supernatural crossovers, set in modern Baton Rouge. It shows how I reuse ideas, develop a Stage, choose starting forces, and then follow the players when their interests take the campaign somewhere I had not planned.

<!-- (dl (# Reusing an Earlier Idea)) -->

Before this campaign, I had developed another game around a hidden Nephandi, a spirit beneath the city, and the idea of Time becoming a force. That campaign fell through for reasons unrelated to the game. I liked much of what had happened in the preparation and play, though, so I used it as inspiration rather than abandoning the material.

The earlier campaign was a separate game, and its starting forces were different. The Mokolé were one of those forces, openly pursuing their goal of buying land. The Vampires had competing interests, and the players' investigation into a massacre helped set off a real-estate war between them. That is the war I refer to in Chapter 10. The Baton Rouge campaign in this example borrows ideas from that game, but it is not the same campaign.

I did not carry over a finished plot. I took pieces that interested me and built a new Stage around them. Some details came from Baton Rouge itself. The city is known as Red Stick because, according to local history, settlers found the area marked by sticks with red tops, placed there by Native people. No one knows what the sticks meant or why they were there.

In my Stage, the Mokolé had placed the sticks to mark the resting place of a minor reptile Incarna. That gave the real-world mystery a supernatural history and connected the land to a group whose present-day interests could grow from it.

<!-- (dl (# Building the Stage Through Its History)) -->

I gave Baton Rouge a recent supernatural history that shaped the city before the characters arrived. Cane awoke for twenty-four hours and followed Cane's law, killing the worst offenders he encountered. At the time, Johnathan Harker, a young Euthanatos protégé who was blood bound to the Prince, used his contacts to form an army intended to kill Cane. They failed. In the aftermath, the vampires, mages, and werewolves in Baton Rouge were dead.

One werewolf survived: Johnathan's best friend, a Ronin Glass Walker whose tribal name was Jester. Jester had been unable to convince anyone that the attack was futile, so he ran. Around the same time, a Rokea who had been living on land fled at the thought that Cane was coming to town. She was careless, captured by her own kind, and killed. Shortly after these events, the Avatar Storm happened.

Johnathan had also been taken under the wing of a respected Celestial Chorister. The Chorister was secretly a Nephandi, and Johnathan was on his way to becoming one too, though he did not know it. These connections meant that the city's past contained both a public catastrophe for its supernatural population and a hidden personal history that could still affect the present.

The Mokolé's mark in the landscape also mattered in modern Baton Rouge. With other supernatural creatures absent for more than twenty years, the Mokolé were trying to buy as much land as possible. Their aim was to have the federal government declare Baton Rouge tribal lands. The same history that explained the sticks therefore created a present-day political goal.

At first, I had focused too much on supernatural events. I realized that the disappearance of the city's supernatural population should also have natural, visible consequences. Without vampires controlling the underworld, it had grown rich and diverse. Control of that underworld had become desirable, and a gang war was underway. The mayor was campaigning on a “War Against Crime” platform.

Adding these consequences gave the Stage more than supernatural mysteries. It created everyday pressures, public politics, and criminal conflict that could affect the characters whether or not they knew the supernatural history behind them. The change also gave me more possibilities for forces: individual gang leaders and the mayor could each have become forces, alongside the Mokolé and supernatural actors.

<!-- (dl (# Choosing the Starting Forces)) -->

I consolidated the gangs into one openly active force. They were not a single organization, but together they were trying to control Baton Rouge. Their goal was visible in the gang war; their fingerprint was disorganization and internal conflict, the instability of several groups acting toward a shared goal without being unified.

The Nephandi was another starting force, but unlike the gangs, he was secretive. He was likable and charismatic, and obsessively careful not to be discovered. His fingerprint was the lack of a fingerprint: when he acted, there was a strange void around who or what had caused it. His goal was to corrupt the Incarna and then kill it, creating a Nexus of corruption in the heart of Baton Rouge.

My third starting force was Time, carried forward from the earlier game's ideas. Time wanted to free Jester from survivor's guilt. The Mokolé and the mayor remained part of the Stage, but I did not make them part of the starting trio. I had a populated situation with more possible actors than I needed to make active forces at once.

<!-- (dl (# Starting with a Collision)) -->

The inciting incident came from a collision between the gangs and the Nephandi. The gangs were disposing of evidence near one of his corruption sites. If they continued, they might draw attention to what he was doing to the spirit. He arranged for them to fight, and the conflict became a shootout in a Taco Bell where the characters were having lunch.

The characters were caught in the fallout of actions they had not caused and did not yet understand. The opening did not require them to know about the Incarna, the Nephandi, or the gang's evidence. The collision gave them an immediate situation to respond to, while the reasons behind it could emerge through play.

<!-- (dl (# Following the Players into the Gang War)) -->

The players focused on the gangs. I had used conflicts with them to show their brutality, but in each case a gang leader did something that angered a player. The players used their creativity and Magick to kill that leader. Then another gang leader died in a similar way.

Those deaths were not planned by me. They were consequences of what the players chose to do. After the second, though, the pattern felt too coincidental to leave unexplained. I added a fourth force: a hit man hired to destabilize the gangs.

The hit man's fingerprint was a method rather than a calling card. He, she, or it arranged situations so that someone else had a reason to kill the intended target. The gang leaders' deaths remained the players' actions; the new force gave the repeated pattern a cause inside the world without taking those choices away from them.

This is where fast thinking and improvisation paid off. In one scene, the characters worked with a security guard who gave them information. Later, I put the same person in the background as a janitor cowering in the corner. I used that moment to let the characters recognize him and to establish a second fingerprint for the hit man: he was always present. It was a sudden way to introduce the new force, and the players began looking for him in later scenes. Eventually, he offered them his services. Later, the characters captured him. By then, they were powerful enough that he was no longer a force.

The janitor reveal made the new force feel as if he had been woven through the campaign all along, even though I had only just decided what the pattern meant. That is the kind of moment I want this style of preparation and improvisation to make possible: the players experience a solid, connected world, and the connection arrives as a surprise we can enjoy together.

<!-- (dl (# Letting New Forces Emerge)) -->

The hit man had been hired by a force I had not created at the campaign's start: a cartel daughter who wanted to destabilize the gangs so she could take over their members and build one super gang. The players discovered her under the moniker “Chekhov's Gun.” The name amused me because of how she had come to exist: I had introduced the hit man to explain a pattern created by the players' choices, and that in turn led to the person who had hired him.

The players eventually joined Chekhov's Gun. They thought she was crazy, but she was one of only two people they believed they could trust. The other was the Nephandi. That was surprising to me. I knew what his hidden goal was, but the players judged trust from their characters' experiences and made their own choice.

I did not discourage them from killing gang leaders, steer them toward another force, or try to make them distrust the people they chose to trust. I followed their interest and leaned into it—a “yes, and” approach. When the same kind of death happened again, I asked what might make sense in the world. The answer added depth without undoing what the players had done. Their interest in the gang conflict led to a hit man, the hit man led to Chekhov's Gun, and the characters' own judgments led them to ally with her.

<!-- (dl (# What This Example Shows Me)) -->

The campaign did not unfold from a prepared plot. The Stage gave me history, relationships, and pressures to draw on; the players decided which parts mattered. When their choices created a pattern I had not planned, I added a force that made sense of it without taking their actions away. The campaign felt connected not because I anticipated every turn, but because I built on what the players found interesting.
