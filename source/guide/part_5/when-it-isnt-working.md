<!-- (dl (# Signs the Game Isn't Producing the Intended Experience)) -->

I want the game to leave people energized, engaged, and still thinking about what happened. A session does not have to follow my plans to succeed, but I pay attention when the players and I are not enjoying the game, or when the play at the table is not producing the experience we agreed to pursue.

One quiet session is not enough to diagnose a problem. A player may be thinking, tired, or simply less interested in a particular thread. A thread losing the players' attention is not the same as a player losing interest in the game. I look for patterns in how people respond, and I ask the players how the game could be better. Their feedback helps me notice a problem, but I still need to understand what is behind it before choosing a change.

Sometimes the problem is local. A ruling, encounter, or scene may be slowing play down or making a character feel ineffective. Sometimes the players are engaged with the campaign, but not with the material I expected them to care about. And sometimes the group wants a different kind of game from the one I want to run. Those are different problems; they need different responses.

<!-- (dl (# Distinguishing a Technique Problem from a Mismatch)) -->

When something is not working, I try to determine whether the game needs a different technique or whether we want different experiences. I can change how I present a situation, follow a different thread, or let go of a prepared idea. But I cannot make a genuine mismatch disappear by pushing the players toward my preferred style.

The first time I ran Fate Core, the players made characters and chose concepts that did not fit the kind of game I like to run. After two sessions, neither the players nor I were having fun. I could not see a way to add the elements I wanted without invalidating their choices, so I ended the campaign.

That was not a problem I could solve by steering the characters toward my plans. Doing that would have asked the players to give up the characters they had chosen in order to make the game suit me. Ending the campaign was better than continuing to run a game that was not working for any of us.

The next time I ran Fate Core, I was clearer about the style of game I wanted and developed the Stage before character creation. That gave the players useful material for making characters who could find fun in a genre I would enjoy running too. I had changed what I could control—how I explained the game and how much context I prepared—rather than trying to change the players' choices after the fact.

<!-- (dl (# What to Adjust First)) -->

Before changing the campaign, I listen for what the players are actually responding to. I ask what could be better, consider the reasons behind their suggestions, and look for patterns rather than treating every comment as an instruction to follow literally. I also take my own enjoyment seriously. I am part of the table, and a game that consistently makes the GM unhappy is not a sustainable success.

If the players are engaged but a particular part is not working, I can change that part. I can spend less time on a thread they are not pursuing, make a ruling differently, or prepare a clearer situation for the next session. I do not need to preserve an idea just because I spent time preparing it. The Stage and forces exist to help me respond to play, not to compete with it.

In one campaign, I had prepared an evil wizard who had been corrupting the city for decades, a Vampire Lord who controlled its largest businesses, and a tribe of weregators trying to gain control of the city government for spiritual reasons. The players found the parts of the Stage connected to the wizard much more interesting than the weregators. They began investigating what had happened decades before the game started.

I followed that interest. The campaign became more of a “True Crime” story, and I developed Time as a force: the past appeared through reflections, and Time's goal was retribution for a single sin the wizard had committed. I had not planned for the past to become so important, and Time was not a force I had imagined before play. The players' interest showed me what in the Stage was worth developing.

That was not a case of rescuing a failing campaign by making the players return to the weregators. The game was giving us a direction that interested the players, and my preparation let me build on it. I could let an unused part recede and turn a detail the players cared about into something with agency and consequences.

<!-- (dl (# Repairing Mistakes with the Group)) -->

Sometimes the problem comes from a decision I made. If a ruling or technique is making play less fun, undermining a character, or producing a consequence I did not intend, I can acknowledge that and change how I handle it. I explain what I am changing, preserve what has already happened where I can, and apply the new understanding consistently from then on.

Repair does not mean pretending the problem did not happen. I want to understand what went wrong and what would help the group move forward. A conversation may clarify that I misunderstood what the players wanted, that a technique did not work for them, or that we had different expectations from the start.

Safety is different from ordinary game problems. A player can veto content without explanation, and the offending context and its direct consequences are removed. I do not ask the group to weigh that against continuity, preparation, or anyone's preferred story.

<!-- (dl (# When a Different Approach Is Better)) -->

If the players and I want different kinds of games, we can talk about whether there is an experience we would all enjoy. I can change what I run, the group can choose a different game, or we can decide not to continue together. But if the only way to make the campaign work is to invalidate player choices or make someone at the table keep playing a game they do not enjoy, it may be time to stop.

Ending a campaign is not proof that the GM failed. Sometimes it is the clearest way to respect what the people at the table want. In my first Fate Core campaign, I ended the game when I could not see a way to bring my preferred elements in without taking away the players' choices. The next time, I explained the kind of game I wanted up front and built a Stage that could help everyone find a place in it.

When something is not working, I try to find out what the experience is telling me. If the players are showing me what interests them, I follow that interest. If I have made a mistake, I own it and repair what I can. If our expectations do not fit, I say so rather than forcing the game to continue. The aim is not to save every campaign or every idea; it is to make a game the people at the table can enjoy together.
