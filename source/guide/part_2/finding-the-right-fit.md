<!-- (dl (# Choosing a System for the Experience I Want)) -->

For me, the system is almost inconsequential. You can tell any kind of story with any kind of system. What matters most is that the table will have fun with the system; everything after that can be patched.

That does not mean system choice is meaningless. I recommend that GMs understand what they like and dislike. I personally don't run tactical systems anymore because I don't find them fun. I prefer story-based systems, so I tend to run games such as World of Darkness, Fate, and Cypher. Those are my tastes, not a universal recommendation. Choose a system the table wants to play, then adapt what needs adapting.

<!-- (dl (# Finding Players Who Want That Experience)) -->

I am up front with players about the kind of game I expect to run. I may not reveal the exact theme if doing so would give too much away, but I talk about what kinds of things will be in the story and what parts of play are likely to take focus: combat, narrative, investigation, or some combination.

I also tell players what topics I want to address, especially when those topics might be trigger topics. Players need enough information to decide whether they want to join, even when some details of the story remain a surprise.

<!-- (dl (# Establishing Expectations, Boundaries, and Feedback)) -->

Before play, we decide together what content can go into the game and when it can happen. Every player can veto content at any time, no questions asked. If a veto is used, I remove the offending context, make a note, and after the session ask only whether I recorded it correctly so it can be added to our banned-topics list.

<!-- (dl (## Why the Veto Is Needed)) -->

The veto protects a player's agency and consent during play. Agreeing to join a game, or agreeing beforehand that a topic is acceptable, does not mean agreeing to endure every way that topic might unfold. The player has the final say over whether they continue engaging with content that affects them.

Nobody can be completely self-aware all the time. A player may discover a boundary only when something in play affects them. They may not understand their reaction yet, much less be able to explain it. They do not need to have identified or announced that boundary beforehand for it to matter.

Things happen quickly at the table, and we sometimes respond by instinct. An interaction or improvised detail can accidentally trigger someone even when everyone is acting with care. The veto is not a judgment that the GM was careless or that a player failed to disclose something. Nobody has to have failed for it to be needed.

It is an escape valve: a way out of that moment and back toward normalcy at the table. An agreed list of banned topics is useful, but it cannot anticipate everything that might affect someone. The veto remains available when a reaction surprises everyone, including the person experiencing it.

"Stop this" is sufficient. The player does not need to understand their reaction, disclose personal experiences, provide proof, defend a boundary, or negotiate for permission. A veto is not debated or put to a vote. I remove the offending context and its direct consequences. If the veto concerns an action by a player character, I work with that player to find another action the character can take. The fiction can change; the person should not have to endure the moment for the game to continue.

<!-- (dl (## Why We Practice Before Play)) -->

I don't start a game with a player until we have practiced vetoing topics twice. The first practice is usually silly: I might say, “Ham is your trigger topic and bread is yours,” then describe a scene that includes one of those topics so everyone can try using a veto. I repeat the exercise with a different, real example, such as animal abuse, if it is not already on the forbidden-topics list.

Knowing that a veto is allowed is different from feeling able to use it in a fast-moving or emotionally intense moment. Practice makes it a familiar, available action before someone discovers they need it. Each player gets to rehearse using the veto and experience the table honoring it. I also get to practice stopping and removing the context promptly, without asking for an explanation or turning it into a discussion.

The purpose is to rehearse the procedure, not to test anyone's limits or require personal disclosure. I want using the escape valve to take as little effort as possible when a real moment calls for it.

<!-- (dl (## When Practice Feels Infantilizing)) -->

Some players may find this practice patronizing, especially the silly example. They are adults who understand what "stop" means, and being asked to demonstrate it can feel as though I doubt their competence or am treating them like children. That is a concern worth taking seriously, not evidence that they disregard safety or consent.

Understanding the rule is not what we are practicing. A player can understand it perfectly and still hesitate to interrupt a scene everyone else seems to enjoy, abandon something the GM has prepared, or act on a reaction they cannot yet explain. Experience does not make someone immune to those pressures. Practice gives them a chance to see that using the veto really does stop the content without requiring a defense. It gives them evidence of how this table responds, rather than only my promise about how it will respond.

The rest of us are practicing too. I rehearse stopping and removing the context; everyone else rehearses accepting the interruption without debating it, demanding reasons, or making the person responsible for restoring the mood. The player using the veto is not performing for my approval. We are rehearsing a shared procedure in which that player's decision is sufficient.

Many people have experienced the same reason for rehearsal in workplace safety drills, especially in jobs with specialized safety concerns. Competent adults practice so that an unexpected, stressful moment is not their first attempt at using the procedure, and so everyone knows how to respond when someone signals a problem. The comparison is about why we rehearse, not about equating a game with a hazardous workplace. A drill can itself be patronizing or perfunctory; the analogy does not excuse that here.

That distinction matters. Infantilization would mean treating players as incapable of understanding or managing their own experience and substituting my judgment for theirs. The veto does the opposite: I cannot know their experience for them, so they retain the authority to stop content even when I do not understand why. Practice is meant to make that authority usable, not to assess their maturity.

A worthwhile purpose does not excuse a patronizing presentation. I explain the reason before the exercise, keep it brief, and take part myself rather than adopting the role of a teacher testing students. If the silly example feels demeaning, we can choose a straightforward, low-stakes example instead. The silliness is not the point; rehearsing both the veto and the table's response is.

I might introduce it this way:

> We are not practicing because I think you do not understand the word "stop." We are practicing so you can see what happens when you use it, and so all of us, including me, rehearse responding without making you explain yourself. I do not want the first time we try this together to be a moment when someone actually needs it.

If someone objects, I ask what about the exercise feels patronizing and discuss its format rather than treating the objection as proof that they need instruction. I still require practice before play at my table, but I do not need to insist on a particular performance or joke to accomplish it. The aim is to establish a trustworthy response together, not to demand that players prove they can follow directions.
