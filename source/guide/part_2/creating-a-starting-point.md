<!-- (dl (# What I Need to Know Before Play Begins)) -->

I think of campaign creation as building a physics engine: create a situation with Forces that can move the world, then let the characters enter and change what happens. I need to understand the situation well enough to respond to surprises, not know what story the players will produce. The Stage gives Forces a world to act in; collisions between them start Arcs that develop through play.

<!-- (dl (## Begin with a Theme)) -->

Before I develop the campaign, I decide what it is about. I call that its theme: a question, such as “Can you hunt monsters without becoming one?”, or an idea I want to explore. A question opens up possibilities rather than supplying an answer; the campaign's situations give players chances to explore what it means to their characters.

What makes a theme good for this approach is not how clever or open-ended its wording sounds, but whether this GM and these players can keep interacting with it. A fruitful theme keeps giving the GM ways to create situations around it and gives the players ways to explore it through their characters' choices. It does not run out after one dilemma or one obvious answer; the changing campaign can bring it forward from new angles. “Good versus evil” may feel settled and offer little to a particular group, but for a group that wants to question what good and evil mean, it can sustain rich play. The right theme depends on what the people at the table find meaningful to explore.

Theme is not a label I attach after creation. It guides how I develop the Stage, Forces, their goals and fingerprints, their collisions, and their fallout, so the whole situation raises the question from different angles. No single detail needs to represent the theme, and no Force needs to stand for one side of an argument. I create varied pressures for the characters to encounter; the players decide what those pressures mean and how to respond.

In a Mage: The Ascension game, my theme was “Can you hunt monsters without becoming one?” I populated the city with a monstrous but not evil Sabbat pack and bishop, a cold and calculating Nosferatu Camarilla prince, a cold war between the two, two different vampire Anarch groups, a werewolf civil war, and a history of vampires mysteriously losing key leaders. No one detail was the theme. Together, they gave “monster” more than one meaning and made the question part of the city rather than a speech the characters had to hear.

The starting forces were an Anarch leader who wanted vampires revealed to the world so it would become a world where only the strong survive; a prince in danger of being removed by the Camarilla because he had only become prince after his predecessor disappeared; and an overworked, understaffed police force fighting to prevent further defunding. The starting collision came from the Anarchs wanting to forcibly recruit a police detective while the Sabbat wanted to kill him. The detective smelled something fishy and sent the characters to investigate. They found themselves between two overwhelming vampire forces, neither interested in fighting them. The opening offered one way into the campaign's question; the wider city and the characters' choices would keep exploring it without deciding what they should do or conclude about the monsters around them.

<!-- (dl (## Forces)) -->

At its core, a Force is a person or group with a goal and more power than the characters in the arena where it pursues that goal, giving it the ability to move the world. A Force is not the same thing as an antagonist: "Force" describes an actor's capacity to affect the situation, while "antagonist" describes its relationship to the characters or their aims. A Force might help the characters, oppose them, or pursue goals that have little to do with them. That relationship can also change over the course of a campaign.

For my purposes, a useful Force has all four of these qualities:

- It is more powerful than the characters in the arena relevant to its goal, and can affect the situation in ways they cannot easily ignore.
- It has a goal it is trying to achieve.
- It has lines it will and will not cross.
- It has a fingerprint: a recognizable signature in how it acts.

The comparison is specific to the arena where the Force pursues its goal; it does not have to be more powerful than the characters in every way. A mayor may have far greater influence over city policy while being no match for the characters in a fight. A church may have a food network and community trust the characters lack, while depending on them in another arena. A Force's power might come from political authority, money, public trust, specialized knowledge, social reach, supernatural influence, or control of something people need. What matters is that it can make things happen in pursuit of its goal at the scale relevant to the campaign.

A fingerprint is the Force's signature in the way it acts: its characteristic methods, habits, priorities, or way of treating people. I borrow the idea from children's cartoons, where a hero or villain has a signature way of doing things that makes them recognizable—even in disguise. I use a Force's fingerprint to make its actions feel as if they come from that particular actor and belong in the same world. When I decide what the Force does, I let its signature shape the action, rather than merely adding a clue or calling card afterward. The players may then connect separate events, recognize a disguised Force, or learn which fingerprints they trust or fear. Those are valuable effects, but the fingerprint's first job is to make the Force's behavior feel coherent and distinctive. A force can be an individual or a group; I can treat a group as one character for its shared goal while still treating its members as individuals.

The theme helps me choose goals, methods, and fingerprints that matter in this campaign. A Force can bring one pressure to bear; its relationships and collisions with others can bring out another. I do not make Forces into mouthpieces for a moral position.

A prepared fingerprint is provisional. If a different signature emerges in play, I record and use that pattern rather than forcing the original idea into later scenes.

Being a force does not mean being mysterious or hidden. A force may be publicly known and pursue its goals openly, or conceal its identity or intentions. A fingerprint can make a known Force's behavior distinctive, or give the players a way to recognize it when its identity is concealed.

Forces do not have to be villains or even obvious sources of conflict. A church might organize kitchens and food deliveries because it wants to end hunger. A mayor might court voters and redirect city resources because they want to be reelected. A police chief, haunted by the violence of the streets, might want people to feel safe and use the department's authority to pursue that goal. Each can change the situation through the work it does, the people it brings together, and the choices it makes. Their goals may align with the characters', conflict with them, or simply intersect; good intentions do not guarantee agreement, but neither do they make opposition inevitable.

I also create supporting characters. They do not need to meet the criteria for a force. They can connect the characters to forces, show the effects of those forces, or carry a thematic or emotional thread of their own.

As a rule of thumb, I start with three forces. I give them goals that do not directly oppose one another. Their actions may bring them into conflict, but they may also support or complicate one another, or remain unrelated. Their purposes do not need to be opposite sides of the same struggle.

For my purposes, directly opposing goals simplify two forces down to a single force: everything they do interacts. Separate goals give me more possibilities for how their actions might intersect, whether that produces conflict, cooperation, or no immediate connection.

<!-- (dl (### When There Are Too Many Forces in Play)) -->

There is no fixed maximum number of Forces. The problem is not how many people or groups exist in the world, but how many I need to actively track and bring into play at once. A Stage can contain many possible actors while only a few are currently pursuing goals that matter to the characters.

I may have too many active Forces when I can no longer keep their goals, limits, and fingerprints distinct; when I have to force connections just to use them; or when their competing developments crowd out the situations the players are trying to engage with. The players may also be hearing about more actors and conflicts than they can make sense of, while Arcs multiply faster than they can follow. More preparation in that state can leave me with more notes but less confidence about what would plausibly happen next.

When I notice these signs, I do not need to delete Forces from the world. I can let some recede from the foreground and bring them back when the characters' actions or interests make them relevant. A useful check is: if the characters do something unexpected now, can I tell which Forces would notice, care, and act—and why? If too many seem equally urgent, or I cannot distinguish their likely responses, I should narrow my active focus.

<!-- (dl (### When There Are Too Few Forces in Play)) -->

There is no fixed minimum number of Forces. The question is whether enough independent actors are active to make the Stage move and give the characters meaningful situations to affect. Too few may leave one Force carrying more than its goals and limits can support. If the situation has little motion without that Force, or I have to invent pressure from outside it, the Stage may be functioning as a backdrop rather than a system. When an Arc ends, there may also be no other active goal or developing situation to catch the players' interest.

Too few active fingerprints can make the campaign feel repetitive even when the characters' choices technically change events. If different situations keep unfolding through the same methods, priorities, and kinds of consequences, the world seems to answer in the same voice. Players may begin to feel bored, or that their actions do not matter, because the results all feel alike.

When I notice these signs, I can add or activate another Force, or give an existing actor room to pursue an independent goal. I do not need to add more Forces simply to increase the count; I need enough distinct agency and fingerprints for the situation to produce varied, connected developments. A useful check is: if the current Arc ended or the players ignored it, what else in this part of the world would still be changing?

<!-- (dl (## The Stage)) -->

The Stage is the background of the campaign: its props, places, stories, and relationships. It explains why the forces I have chosen are the forces that matter here. It also needs enough life and movement that other forces can emerge. I develop the Stage so its history, relationships, and pressures help the campaign keep raising its thematic question. No place or piece of history has to stand for the theme by itself; together, they give the forces' actions context and give that context meaning.

Most games already have a canon that describes a populated world. That is usually my starting place, not something I need to replace. My Stage describes some portion of that world and adds enough depth that it feels alive.

I map relationships between forces, including alliances, rivalries, obligations, history, and dependencies. I consider how people and local groups view them: who loves a force, who hates it, and who would hate it if they knew its identity. I distinguish what people know from what they believe, including what the forces know or believe about one another. These notes can be light; a sentence or three may be enough to give me something useful.

The Stage and forces develop together. A force gives me reasons to build parts of the Stage, and those parts suggest new forces. Often I begin play with different forces from the ones that originally inspired me because better options emerge during development. Neither exists independently of the other.

In the campaign with the Nephondi High Mage, he was the initial force. I needed a Stage that allowed him to exist and pursue his goal. That led to the spirit beneath the city. Once I had the spirit, I realized I needed guardians. Because the campaign was set in Baton Rouge, that development led to a were-gator tribe becoming another force.

This connected work is where most of my initial development goes. I may produce dozens of ideas for possible forces, but that is a side effect. I only need three to begin.

<!-- (dl (### When the Stage Is Not Developed Enough)) -->

The Stage may not be developed enough when I keep having to invent foundational details during play instead of drawing on what I have established. I may also struggle to picture ordinary life there: not just the campaign's central conflict, but what people do and how the place feels when that conflict is not in the foreground.

Another sign is that I often find myself without an answer to what the players do. I do not need to know their choices or decide outcomes in advance. I need enough context—people, relationships, history, and pressures—to make a grounded next move when they surprise me. If I cannot see what could plausibly follow from their actions, the Stage may need more development.

<!-- (dl (### When the Stage Is Overdeveloped)) -->

I look for two signs that I have developed the Stage too much:

- **I struggle to find what matters in my own material.** The volume of detail makes the Stage harder to use rather than helping me respond.
- **Development delays play.** I keep preparing instead of starting, even though I already understand the situation well enough to run it.

Unused detail is not itself a problem; it gives me possibilities to draw on. Development becomes excessive when it obstructs access to what matters or keeps the game from beginning. Attachment to prepared material is a different problem: I can resist changing or leaving out details even in a small Stage.

<!-- (dl (## When the Stage Is Ready)) -->

I use four criteria to decide when I have developed the Stage enough:

1. **I am bored with working on it.** I have fiddled and played with it enough that I do not want to keep doing that.
2. **I cannot think of another addition that is both important and interesting for the characters.** Just because something might never be used is no excuse for it to be boring.
3. **It makes sense.** I can understand why everything is there and how each piece affects the others.
4. **I can envision a populated world emerging from it.** It feels like a living portion of the setting, not just a collection of isolated ideas.

The fourth criterion is difficult to explain, especially when the game's existing setting already supplies a populated world. I am not trying to create every person in it. I want the part I have developed to add depth and feel alive within that world.

<!-- (dl (# What I Deliberately Leave Undecided)) -->

Most of what I create for the Stage never appears directly in play. That is useful: it gives me possibilities to draw on when surprised, without turning preparation into a list of things the characters must encounter. I leave their decisions, the sequence of events, and the ending open.

<!-- (dl (## Arcs and Stories)) -->

I use "Arc" and "Story" interchangeably. A collision starts an Arc: the events that fall out of that collision belong to the same narrative device. That device can end, even while the campaign continues.

Players may talk about the Forces, but when choosing what to pursue, they engage with Arcs: the situations and developing stories that grow from the Forces' actions. Forces are the engine that creates those situations; the Arc is the main unit of player engagement. I prepare Forces so they can produce Arcs the players want to engage with.

An Arc ends in one of two ways:

- The players stop engaging with it.
- Its fallout reaches a natural conclusion based on character actions.

A natural ending gives the table a sense of having completed something. In rulesets that award experience for completing stories or similar milestones, it also provides a natural point to gain XP.

I suggest having three Arcs in play, introducing them across three sessions rather than starting everything at once. That staggered start lets complexity grow and gives the campaign a sense of depth. It is a rule of thumb, not a schedule I protect from play. I am willing to change it, or anything else, when play calls for that.

The important distinction is that the three Arcs begin with three different collisions involving three different pairs of goals. They are not simply three threads from one collision. As another rule of thumb, I involve no more than two forces in each collision.

The same forces can participate in different Arcs. It is possible for the same pair of forces to collide over different pairs of goals, but I do not suggest that as the starting arrangement.

<!-- (dl (## Preparing a Collision)) -->

Before an Arc starts, I know:

- The two forces involved.
- The goals driving their actions.
- What caused the conflict.
- The combatants.

For the first combat of the game, I also know where it takes place and whether I intend it to be a victory or a defeat for the characters. Otherwise, the location remains undecided. Everything else is open.

For example, consider these three forces:

- **Force A** wants to become mayor.
- **Force B** wants to bring all crime in the city under its control.
- **Force C** wants to remove its biggest business competitor from the city.

None of those goals directly opposes another. The collisions come from what the forces do to pursue them:

1. **A and C collide over a strike.** C has caused a strike, and A uses local authorities to break it up to gain votes in the upcoming election.
2. **B and C collide over a drive-by.** C's beefed-up private security stops a drive-by near its business that would have eliminated a gang leader.
3. **A and B collide through public violence.** A gang leader's daughter works for A. B uses bribed authorities to take out that leader with lethal force at a conference for A.

These can start three distinct Arcs using only three forces.

The conference is an example of where the event might happen, not a place I need to push the characters toward. Before that session, I only need to know that B will oppose A through violence in a public space because the gang leader's daughter works for A. I choose the public space based on where the characters go.

That is how I leave later collisions flexible enough to weave into character actions. I prepare the conflict, not the characters' route to it.

<!-- (dl (# How Characters Connect to the Game)) -->

Usually, I do most of this development after the table has formed but before we meet to make characters. Character creation then gives me details to backfill into the Stage.

There is no fixed amount of information I give the players before character creation. It depends on the theme and the particular game. Knowing the theme, Stage, initial forces, and opening collision gives me material to decide what the players should know and what their characters should know.

I share a detail when it serves one or more of these purposes:

1. It prevents surprise about the type of game being run.
2. It guides players toward characters who fit the Stage and chosen theme.
3. It helps explain the opening combat afterward.

I keep those details as brief as possible, and no briefer. I want enough information for players to make fitting characters and understand the kind of game they are joining, without making them sit through unnecessary opening rhetoric. Content expectations and safety boundaries still need to be established; reducing exposition does not replace that conversation.

Sometimes I do not know the inciting incident until the characters are made. I have even held off on creating the Stage and forces until then. I do not recommend that unless the GM and players are experienced and have played many games together.

<!-- (dl (# Giving Players Something Worth Engaging With)) -->

<!-- (dl (## The Opening Collision)) -->

I always start the game with combat arising from a collision of forces. The characters do not have to fight the forces directly; they can be caught in the fallout of their actions.

Most games have their most developed rules in combat. Starting there lets the players try their new characters using the part of the game the rules support most heavily. It also focuses the table immediately. I have never had an opening combat fail to engage the players; it was the way I found to begin a campaign with everyone interested.

But the opening combat is the dreaded railroad. The characters must already have made some decision to be in a place where the combat can happen. I am making that starting decision for them, so I do not want to build too much around it. This prepared frame is useful only for the first few moments of the first session; once play is underway, I follow the players' choices rather than treating the opening as a route the campaign must continue to follow.

My rule is: **The decision must be something the players can accept that their characters did before the game started.**

If they cannot accept that premise, I have lost their engagement before combat begins. Preparing an opening situation is not permission to assign them choices that do not fit their characters.

<!-- (dl (## A Non-Lethal First Fight)) -->

As a rule of thumb, the opening combat needs to be guaranteed non-lethal. This is the players' first chance to use their characters, and they are going to assume they will win. Winning does not have to happen, but losing should not mean losing those new characters.

I ensure this through the encounter and its consequences rather than announcing the guarantee beforehand. My goal is to reduce opening rhetoric; the players do not need an explanation of this preparation. They need to see the consequences afterward.

When I intend the opening to be a victory, I make the combat well below the characters' abilities: a sure win. When I intend it to be a loss, the opposition's objective is to capture or drive them away, and the enemies do not use lethal combat techniques.

This is a specific decision about the first fight, not a predetermined outcome for the Arc. The characters' responses and the events that follow remain open.

<!-- (dl (## When Combat Ends in Failure)) -->

The opening fight is a special case, but the principle applies to combat more broadly: **failure in combat should always lead to a more interesting situation.** I take this subrule from *Fate Core*. It applies to losing a combat, not to an individual failed roll.

A character's death can be the right outcome, but it is not automatically the most interesting one. Capture, for example, may give the characters new problems and choices to face. I look for a loss that changes the situation in a way the table can engage with, rather than defaulting to death.

<!-- (dl (## Letting Questions Bring Out the Background)) -->

Once the combat is over, the players start asking questions. Now I can give them the opening explanation because they are seeking it instead of waiting for me to finish talking.

In one campaign, every character had a police officer as a contact. That connection existed before the fight, but afterward I explained that the characters were there because the contact had asked them to look into something. I gave everyone a free advantage because I knew what the opening needed.

The connection made the starting decision plausible for the characters. The combat gave the players something immediate to do. Their questions then gave me a way to explain the situation when they wanted to understand it.

<!-- (dl (# Trying This Approach)) -->

Choose a theme, then develop a Stage and Forces whose connections can generate distinct collisions. Prepare enough for a usable starting situation, including an opening the players can accept, but leave later locations and outcomes open. Share the context players need to make fitting characters, then use their ideas to enrich the Stage.
