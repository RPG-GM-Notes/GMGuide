<!-- (dl (# The Approach)) -->

Begin with a theme: a question or idea the campaign will keep putting into play. Prepare a living situation that lets the table encounter it from different angles—not a story the players must follow. The Stage, Forces, their collisions, and the fallout create opportunities for the characters to explore what the question means to them. No single detail has to represent the theme; the campaign's parts create it together, without deciding the answer for the players.

<!-- (dl (## Build a Small, Connected World)) -->

The **Stage** gives events context and depth: places, people, history, relationships, and pressures. **Forces** pursue goals and have more power than the characters in the relevant arena, letting them move the world within that Stage. This power may come from authority, resources, trust, knowledge, or other means; it need not apply in every arena. A Force is not necessarily an antagonist: a church working to end hunger or a mayor seeking reelection can shape events just as much as an obvious enemy. Give each Force a recognizable signature in how it acts—its methods, habits, priorities, or way of treating people. Let that fingerprint shape its actions so the Force feels distinctive and consistent; players may then recognize it, even in disguise, and learn which patterns they trust or fear. A fingerprint does not mean the Force must be hidden. Their actions and the characters' choices change the situation. The parts matter through their connections; a force without a Stage has little to give its actions meaning.

Use the theme to shape the Stage, the forces, their goals and fingerprints, the situations they create, and the way you describe them, so that these parts together keep raising the thematic question from different angles. Develop the Stage and forces together. A force may suggest what the Stage needs, while a detail in the Stage may reveal a new force. Prepare enough to understand the situation, not every possible event.

Forces drive events, but Arcs—the situations and developing stories their actions create—are the main unit players engage with. Prepare Forces to generate Arcs the players want to pursue.

<!-- (dl (## Let Play Create the Story)) -->

At the table, repeat this loop:

1. Present what is happening.
2. Find out what the players want their characters to do.
3. Decide what, if anything, needs resolution.
4. Establish what changes.
5. Give the situation back to the players.

Use the Stage, forces, goals, limits, established events, and theme to decide what follows. When several consequences could naturally arise, choose one that offers a meaningful angle on the thematic question. Let the players' choices stand. Follow what interests them; do not force them toward a prepared thread or a moral conclusion. Consequences should grow from the situation and give the players something meaningful to respond to, not secretly cancel their success or imply a preferred answer.

<!-- (dl (## Keep the Table's Priorities Clear)) -->

- **Safety is the hard boundary.** A player's veto is not debated or put to a vote.
- **The table's fun comes first.** Change or discard a plan, character, rule, or system that gets in the way.
- **Player choices matter.** Preparation provides possibilities, not a route they must take.
- **Continuity serves the experience.** Keep the world understandable, but do not protect literal consistency at the cost of safety or agency.
- **Adapt rules of thumb.** The number of forces, preparation habits, and opening techniques are tools, not universal requirements.

<!-- (dl (## Prepare to Improvise)) -->

Before play, decide what question or idea the campaign will keep putting into play. Build enough of the Stage and forces for their details, relationships, and actions to create opportunities to explore it from different angles. Between sessions, note what changed and prepare the fallout and material the characters may need to engage with. Leave their choices, conclusions, and outcomes open.

This preparation helps improvisation feel coherent. Players may experience the world as complete and connected even when you make a connection in the moment. The goal is not to fool them; it is to create a shared moment of wonder.
