<!-- (dl (# Why I'm Writing This)) -->

Much of the campaign-creation advice I've encountered seems to lead to campaigns that feel dead to me: either the game is pushed along a predetermined track, or the GM is left stranded when the characters do something nobody could have predicted. I don't want to predict what the characters will do. I want enough understanding of the situation to make decisions in real time that feel like they belong in the same world and story.

This guide is my attempt to capture how I do that. I prepare heavily at the beginning, spending about 70% of that preparation building the Stage and about 20% building the forces: a small number of people or groups with goals, limits, and recognizable fingerprints. I consider how the forces relate to one another and to the characters. I prepare much less after play begins. That initial work gives me a model of the situation, not a sequence of events I expect the players to follow.

In that sense, a campaign is a system. The Stage is its environment: the places, histories, relationships, and pressures that give action meaning. The forces are its active agents: they want something, they have limits, and they can move the world. The characters are another set of agents entering that system and changing its behavior. The campaign is not a machine I power by telling the players what happens next. It is a network of relationships and pressures that produces consequences when the players act on it.

At the table, I use that model to improvise. The forces pursue their goals; the characters interfere, cooperate, or do something I never anticipated; and the situation changes in response. A campaign works best when its parts have enough internal logic that the resulting behavior feels understandable even when it is surprising. I try to follow what the players find entertaining without deciding outcomes in advance. The aim is improvisation that stays coherent: the world can surprise us without feeling arbitrary, and the story can change without losing its continuity or tone.

<!-- (dl (# What This Guide Will—and Won't—Teach)) -->

This is a style of play built around a substantial initial investment and low-maintenance preparation afterward. It is meant to help a GM follow the joy of the table and improvise freely, while giving them enough understanding to keep the campaign feeling cohesive.

It is not a formula for producing a successful campaign. The ideas here are rules of thumb, not laws. They can—and should—be changed to fit the circumstances, and understanding why a rule of thumb exists matters more than following it mechanically. This approach cannot guarantee that a game will be fun, remove the GM's responsibility for story design, or prevent every improvised choice from disrupting continuity or even breaking the story. It offers tools that can help prevent those problems and help a GM respond when the unexpected happens.

<!-- (dl (# Who This Approach Might Suit)) -->

I'm writing this first to capture my own thoughts so I can review and refine them. Eventually, I hope to publish the guide freely on the web. I especially hope it will help new Gamemasters, and that some of its techniques will inspire the people writing books about running games.

This approach may suit a GM who enjoys responding to players more than steering them toward a planned sequence, but still wants the world to feel consistent. It may also suit someone willing to spend time developing a campaign before play in exchange for having less to prepare between sessions. It won't be the right fit for every GM or every table; the point is to understand the approach well enough to decide what is useful for yours.

<!-- (dl (# How to Use This Guide)) -->

Skim the guide first to get a sense of the whole approach, then read more deeply wherever a chapter is useful to you. You don't need to adopt every rule of thumb. As you read, consider why I use each one, what it is meant to accomplish, and what might need to change for your group.

The examples show what these ideas have looked like in my games, not what every campaign should look like. I always begin with combat created by the collision of forces' actions. The characters may be caught in the fallout rather than fighting those forces directly. That is one way I put the campaign in motion; the larger principle is to create a situation with energy and connections, then let play determine what happens next.
