<!-- (dl (section-meta 3. The Principles Behind My Decisions)) -->

<!-- (dl (# My Core Principles)) -->

<!-- (dl (## Safety is the hard boundary)) -->

The table decides what content is acceptable, and every player can veto content at any time without explanation. Nothing about a plot, encounter, or character takes priority over that.

<!-- (dl (## The table's fun comes first)) -->

The table's fun is the one thing I will not sacrifice. Plot, characters, NPCs, and even the game system are all things I can change or discard if they get in the way. I am part of the table, too, so I look for a kind of play we can enjoy together.

<!-- (dl (## Theme gives the campaign meaning)) -->

Theme is the question or idea the campaign keeps putting into play. I develop the Stage, Forces, their collisions, and the fallout so that, together, they let the characters encounter it from different angles. When deciding what happens next, I first ask, “What is the most interesting outcome that can happen within the theme?” Then I work out how the established situation can bring it about. No single detail has to represent the theme; the campaign's parts create opportunities to explore it through play. The characters' choices let us see what the question means to them. I do not use theme to dictate a lesson or decide the answer. Safety, the table's fun, and the players' freedom to choose remain essential.

<!-- (dl (## Prepare situations, not outcomes)) -->

I put substantial effort into the initial campaign concepts, especially the forces: what they want, what they will and will not do, and how their actions can be recognized. I use this preparation to understand the situation, not to predict what the characters will do or dictate what must happen.

Theme guides that preparation by giving it a question to explore. I use it to shape the Stage, the forces and their goals, their fingerprints, and the conflicts their actions create, so their details work together to raise that question in play. It does not prescribe what the characters must do or believe.

<!-- (dl (## Let play change the situation)) -->

The characters' actions matter. Forces pursue their goals, react to interference, and may change as the campaign develops. I follow what the table finds engaging rather than insisting that players pursue every prepared thread.

<!-- (dl (## Preserve the Appearance of Continuity)) -->

Continuity is not important for its own sake; what matters is its appearance. I want events and character actions to fit the players' understanding of the world and its theme, so that changes do not break that understanding or make the campaign's meaning feel arbitrary. I can engineer an explanation backward when needed. Often the players will make those connections for me, filling in how a new development fits what they already know.

There is a kind of magic in this. Players can experience the world as solid and complete, and the GM as an extraordinary planner, even when the GM is improvising. The goal is not to fool the players about what was prepared in advance. Like a magician creating a moment of wonder, I want us to share an experience that feels magical and memorable. The preparation gives me enough understanding to respond coherently; play supplies the unexpected connections.

<!-- (dl (## Treat the GM as a steward)) -->

I technically decide how forces and the world react, but I think of myself as the table's steward, not as an authority standing outside it. My decisions should help the group have a good experience, not protect my plans from the players.

<!-- (dl (# How These Principles Affect Play)) -->

I prefer encounters to arise from the fallout of character actions rather than being inserted as separate events. In tactical games, I prepare spreads of enemies for different encounter types and reskin them to fit the situation that actually arises.

<!-- (dl (# When Principles Conflict)) -->

Safety is the hard boundary; within it, the table's fun comes first. Continuity helps players trust the world, but I will revise details rather than let literal consistency undermine player choice or enjoyment.

<!-- (dl (# Where My Approach Differs from Familiar Advice)) -->

The advice I have the biggest problem with is advice that starts by having the GM create encounters as separate units. Encounters are important, but I find they work better when they flow naturally from the fallout of character actions. That way, the encounter belongs to the developing situation instead of interrupting it.

This is more difficult in tactical games such as D&D, where encounters may need carefully composed enemy groups. Preparing adaptable enemy spreads lets me keep that tactical structure without deciding in advance when or why an encounter will occur.
