<!-- (dl (# What I Want Players to Experience)) -->

I want the game to feel like a good carnival ride: adrenaline, exhaustion, excitement, and real emotion. I want everyone at the table to have a good time.

My biggest sign that a game has worked is what the players do when it ends. Either they don't want to leave, or they leave rehashing what happened. That energy—the desire to stay in the experience or to keep talking about it afterward—is what I look for.

<!-- (dl (## I Design Systems, Not Stories)) -->

I build a world with enough pressure and meaning for the players' choices, the forces' actions, and the Stage's tensions to create the story in play.

<!-- (dl (# What Makes a Session Feel Successful)) -->

A session is successful when the players are engaged enough to want more, or when the events keep mattering to them afterward. It can feel cohesive even when the characters surprise me and change the situation.

<!-- (dl (# What I Prioritize When I Can't Have Everything)) -->

The table's fun is the only thing I will not sacrifice. Everything else can give way: the plot, the characters, the NPCs, the current TTRPG system, and more.

If the dice are getting in the way, I throw them out and play make-believe instead. Whatever works for the table is more important than continuing to use a rule or tool that is making the game less fun.

<!-- (dl (# What I'm Comfortable Leaving Out)) -->

I'm comfortable leaving out or changing anything that gets in the way of the table's fun. I don't need to preserve a planned plot, protect an NPC from the consequences of play, or keep using a system just because we started with it.

This doesn't mean I don't care about continuity, characters, or the story. I use preparation and the campaign's forces to help keep events coherent while improvising. But those are ways to support the experience, not obligations that outrank it.
